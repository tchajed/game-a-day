# Upper Management

## Concept

**Upper Management** is a five-minute typing game about accuracy and ravens.

The player is the only human employee working late in a high-rise office. A raven—their immediate supervisor—perches beside the keyboard and dictates memos addressed to Upper Management. Nobody acknowledges that this arrangement is unusual.

As the player completes sentences, its corporate euphemisms become real in the office and surrounding skyline. Sentences that have an effect are mixed in with others that are ordinary, or ominous. Mistakes attract attention: the supervisor stops dictating and stares, then more ravens appear and join it. By the end of the shift, the city itself has become part of the company.

The ravens do not need a rational explanation. Their presence should be treated as ordinary corporate procedure, making the situation funnier and more unsettling.

## Core Experience

The game has one primary mechanic: type the dictated memo as accurately as possible.

- The supervisor raven presents one sentence at a time.
- Correct characters appear on the memo.
- A wrong key flashes red, adds a blot to the memo when corrected, and permanently counts against the player's accuracy.
- The player must backspace over every visible wrong key before continuing. Follow-on typing compounds the error into a visible run of bad characters, so each extra character must be erased deliberately.
- Completing a sentence enacts its contents in the environment.
- Every mistake causes a visible reaction from the ravens.
- There's a persistent timer and wpm counter but these are never acknowledged or relevant to the game.
- The full experience should last five minutes, so there should be at least 400 words worth of text.

The player's accuracy controls how quickly the ravens' scrutiny escalates, but every player sees the main environmental progression and reaches the ending if they do all the typing.

## Progression

### 1. Routine Operations

> Please be advised that the western offices will remain dark for the duration of the migration.

The western tower goes dark floor by floor. Something continues moving behind its windows.

The supervisor is initially businesslike. On the first mistake, it stops dictating and turns to stare directly at the player.

### 2. Personnel Policy

> Employees who cast unfamiliar shadows should report themselves to Human Resources.

Shadows detach from office workers and move toward the elevators. Their owners continue working.

Additional mistakes cause ravens to appear on distant rooftops and cables.

### 3. Building Access

> Exterior management may enter through any available window.

Ravens begin landing on neighboring buildings. One appears outside the player's window and taps the glass after a mistake.

### 4. Organizational Restructuring

> All unoccupied desks are to be filled by members of Upper Management.

The dark office behind the player gradually fills with ravens. Whenever the player makes a mistake, every visible raven turns toward the camera in unison.

### 5. Citywide Expansion

> Effective immediately, the surrounding skyline will be considered part of the corporate campus.

Corporate logos illuminate across every building. Windows rearrange into repeating geometric grids. Enormous raven silhouettes pass between the towers.

The perspective begins to feel subtly impossible: buildings lean inward, upper floors extend too far, and the skyline appears to watch the player.

### 6. Promotion Notice

> Your successful transition into management has been approved.

The supervisor gives a satisfied nod and flies away. The camera pulls backward through the office window, revealing that a raven is now sitting at the player's desk. It types the final period.

## Raven Behavior

The supervisor raven should feel like an impatient but restrained manager rather than a monster. Its reactions provide most of the game's personality.

- Taps its beak to introduce the next sentence
- Checks a tiny clipboard or stack of memo slips
- Ruffles its feathers after a mistake
- Slowly leans into the player's space
- Glances toward the other ravens before they all stare together
- Gives a curt, satisfied nod when a memo is completed accurately

Mistake escalation:

1. The supervisor pauses and looks at the player.
2. A distant raven lands on a neighboring antenna.
3. More ravens appear along roofs and cables.
4. A raven lands outside the office window.
5. Ravens begin appearing at desks behind the player.
6. Every raven snaps its head toward the player after an error.
7. A raven becomes visible in the dark reflection of the monitor.

## Visual Direction

The scene is a stylized 2.5D corporate office overlooking a dense city.

- An isometric or lightly perspective-shifted desk and office in the foreground
- Floor-to-ceiling windows framing a deep parallax skyline
- Layered towers with many individually controllable windows
- Procedural raven silhouettes with a few expressive poses
- Navy, charcoal, concrete gray, and fluorescent beige as the main palette
- Red error flashes as the only strong accent color
- The memo displayed on an in-world monitor or sheet of paper on the desk
- A slow camera push toward the window as the game becomes more sinister
- Environmental changes that happen while the player types, not in separate cutscenes

The office should begin mundane and readable. Unease comes from accumulation: missing reflections, detached shadows, synchronized birds, impossible architecture, and the gradual loss of empty space.

## Audio Direction

Audio should remain sparse so each typing mistake feels conspicuous.

- Mechanical keyboard clicks for correct characters
- A dull, incorrect key sound for mistakes
- Fluorescent hum and distant HVAC noise
- Quiet wingbeats and claws against glass
- Beak taps from the supervisor
- A low city drone that deepens as each memo is completed
- Brief silence whenever all the ravens stare

## Tone

The writing should combine dry corporate language with surreal consequences. Memos never describe events emotionally; they frame disappearances, shadows, migration, and occupation as routine policy updates.

The game should be funny at first, sinister by the midpoint, and visually overwhelming by the end. It should never explain why the ravens run the company or how the memos alter reality.
